Rising butt hinge simulator

A rising butt hinge has a helical ramp inside its knuckle, like a short screw thread. As the door opens it rides up the ramp and lifts a few millimetres. Let go, and its weight slides it back down the ramp until it's closed. No spring, no tilted frame.

Rising angle is the slope of the helical ramp. A steeper ramp closes harder but needs more force to open. Typical rising butts use about 25–45°, lifting 6–12 mm over 90°.

Batch test (Sharma ABS model)

No two hinges are identical. Each door gets a random friction multiplier M = 1 + α(η − 1) from the Sharma ABS model (a = −10, b = 10, n = 12, mean-normalised), which is always positive.

About the Sharma ABS model

A screw run backwards

The knuckle's helix works like a nut on a steep thread. The door's weight pushes down on the ramp, and the slope turns that push into a closing twist. It works as long as the ramp is steeper than the friction angle.

Same pull at every angle

Unlike an inclined hinge, the pull doesn't fade near 90°. The door comes back even from 170°. The trade-off is that the closing pull near the frame is no stronger than anywhere else.

Fitting tips

Rising butts are handed (left or right), so order the right one. Bevel the top hinge-side edge of the door and leave a top gap of a few mm more than the lift, so the door doesn't rub the frame as it rises. The door also lifts straight off for removal.

How accurate is this?

The ramp is modelled as a single-start helix with constant lead, using the standard screw equation: closing torque = W·r·(tan λ − μ)/(1 + μ·tan λ), plus friction at the pin from the door's sideways pull (hinges 1.6 m apart, 6 mm pin radius). The door is a uniform 0.9 m × 2.0 m panel. Friction coefficients are typical textbook values, not measurements.

Not modelled: latches, seals, wear on the ramp, and the small extra friction while the door is being pushed open. Use it to compare ramp sizes and finishes, then check on the real door with a spring balance at the handle.
